Saudi Arabia

Getting the MISA License Is the Easy Part.

NIKKI ADVISORY May 30, 2026 5 min read

Most companies spend months preparing for Saudi Arabia's regulatory process. Almost none prepare adequately for what comes after it.

Every conversation I have with businesses considering Saudi Arabia starts in the same place: how long will the license take, can we own 100%, what structure makes most sense?

These are fair questions. But they're the wrong ones to obsess over.

The licensing process — handled correctly — is navigable. MISA's investment framework has improved considerably. Foreign ownership restrictions have eased across most sectors. The commercial registration pathway is well-documented. What is far less discussed, and far more consequential, is what happens in the months after approval comes through.

Saudi Arabia's investment momentum right now is real. FDI inflows reached $31.7 billion in 2024 — a 24% increase even as global investment slowed — and Q1 2025 came in 44% higher year-on-year. The Kingdom is targeting $100 billion in annual FDI by 2030, and the infrastructure, regulatory reforms, and sector-opening decisions that support that goal are visible and accelerating. This is not a market you can afford to enter badly or have to re-enter twice.

The companies I've seen struggle in Saudi Arabia were not undone by the licensing process. They were undone by what they hadn't planned for before the license arrived.

Saudization is a business-continuity issue, not just a compliance checkbox.

The Nitaqat program — Saudi Arabia's workforce nationalization scheme — classifies every private-sector employer into tiers based on the percentage of Saudi nationals in their workforce. Your tier determines whether you can renew expatriate work permits, bid on government contracts, update your commercial registration, or extend your General Manager's iqama. A company in the red zone is operationally constrained in ways that directly affect revenue.

What most companies miss is how rapidly the requirements are evolving. In 2025 alone, Saudization quotas were expanded across 269 professions. Marketing and sales roles now carry a 60% Saudi national requirement for eligible companies. Accounting functions face a phased requirement rising from 40% to 70% over five years. Engineering roles carry a 30% threshold. Companies that built their hiring plans without factoring in Nitaqat — or that assumed their sector was exempt — have found themselves needing to restructure teams they spent months assembling.

Banking and payroll readiness takes longer than most timelines allow.

Opening a corporate bank account in Saudi Arabia as a foreign-owned entity requires a fully incorporated local company, complete KYC documentation, and compliance with the Wage Protection System (Mudad) — which mandates that all employee salaries be paid through a registered local bank account. This is not a parallel process you can run after incorporation. For companies trying to hire their first Saudi-based team while still working through account setup, the delays are directly operational. This specific gap routinely pushes launch timelines back by six to eight weeks beyond what was planned.

Structure and permitted activities must be right from the start.

The MISA license and commercial registration define what your company is legally authorised to do in the Kingdom. When the registered business activities don't match actual operations — which happens more often than it should, usually because the company's model evolved between planning and incorporation — the correction process is slow and creates audit exposure in the interim. This is one of the least visible risks in market entry planning and one of the most disruptive to fix.

The Regional Headquarters question is no longer theoretical.

By the first half of 2024, 184 multinational companies had already relocated their regional headquarters to Saudi Arabia. For government-linked contracts and engagement with major Saudi entities, RHQ presence has moved from a preference to a practical requirement in many cases. Companies that planned to serve the Saudi market from their Dubai or Bahrain offices are revisiting that decision.

What separates the companies that scale well in Saudi Arabia from those that spend their first year troubleshooting?

In my experience, it is almost never a licensing issue. It is almost always whether they treated market entry as a registration project or as an operational plan.

  • The best-prepared companies arrive knowing their Nitaqat obligations before they have a single Saudi-based employee.
  • They've mapped their banking and payroll setup in parallel with registration, not after.
  • Their leadership understands what Zakat and VAT compliance look like in practice, not just in theory.
  • And their business model has been tested against what their commercial registration actually permits.

None of this is complicated. But it requires a more deliberate conversation than most companies are having at the planning stage.

Saudi Arabia's investment story is strong and getting stronger. The opportunity is not in question. What is worth examining — honestly, before committing — is whether you're prepared to operate from day one, not just to launch.

The license is a beginning. What you build after it is what determines whether the entry was worth making.
